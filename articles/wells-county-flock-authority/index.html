Who Authorized the Cameras?

Flock and the public’s right to an accountable explanation of government power.

November 9, 2026Brendon R. ColemanCommentary and document analysis
The recordAuthority and fundingPublic participationNext answersSources

Wells County residents should not have to reconstruct the authority behind a surveillance system after it has already been installed.

My concern with Flock cameras has consistently involved the relationship between government power and the people subject to it. Who authorizes the system? Who controls the information? What can residents do when they object?

The October 2026 commissioners’ minutes give those questions a concrete public record. They also show why a claim about usefulness cannot resolve every question about authority.

What the October 5 minutes establish

Resident Vonnetta Zalac asked who authorized the cameras and when, whether Ordinance 2022-04 was followed, who holds final legal authority, and whether residents would have input before additional cameras were installed. The minutes record the following responses. [1]

The sheriff and the funding source
County Attorney Ted Storer said the sheriff authorized the cameras and paid for them through commissary funds. The minutes do not supply an authorization date.
Commissioner consideration
Storer said the issue was not brought before the commissioners.
Final legal authority
Storer was unable to comment on who held final legal authority over installation, funding, and continued operation.
Future public input
Commissioner Jeff Stringer said additional cameras on county roads would receive public input.
Opposition within county government
The minutes record Commissioner Michael Vanover wanting all Flock cameras removed from Wells County.

These distinctions matter. Identifying who made a decision does not establish that every applicable requirement was satisfied. An attorney’s inability to comment does not prove that no legal authority exists. A commissioner’s support for removal does not itself order removal.

The record establishes that residents asked consequential questions and did not receive a complete public explanation at that meeting.

Spending authority and installation authority

Ordinance 2022-04 begins with cameras protecting the historic County Road 300 West bridge. It regulates access to the information from those cameras and then addresses future public camera installations within the commissioners’ jurisdiction. Section 4 requires those installations to be approved by ordinance and subjects the resulting information to the ordinance’s limitations. [2]

Whether the sheriff’s particular installations fall within that jurisdiction is central to the dispute. The text should be examined alongside the governing state law and the actual locations and authorization records. The October minutes do not resolve that legal question.

The funding question also needs precision. The Indiana State Board of Accounts explains that commissary disbursements may occur without an appropriation for purposes allowed by statute. Commissary funding alone therefore does not establish an improper expenditure. [3]

The account that pays for a system does not, by itself, answer every question about the authority to install and operate it.

Residents deserve an explanation connecting the funding authority, the installation authority, the contract, and any applicable county requirements. Those questions should not be collapsed into a single assurance that the sheriff paid for the cameras.

Public participation and the power to act

The county-road qualification in Stringer’s October 5 answer leaves another question: what public process applies to installations along state roads? Road jurisdiction can affect the legal answer. The public’s interest in how surveillance is governed remains. [1]

The October 19 minutes provide further context. They record both Vanover and Stringer supporting removal. Vanover said residents should have a voice and that improved safeguards would not change his position. The Flock discussion contains no motion ordering removal. [4]

That is a meaningful distinction between stated opposition and an action that changes the arrangement. A resident needs to know which official or body can require a change, through what procedure, and with what limits.

Review is a familiar responsibility

The other business in the October 5 minutes is revealing. A tax-software proposal awaited the statutory contract process. Elevator work awaited contract review. Emergency equipment purchases were postponed for consideration together. [1]

Those purchases can involve different legal rules. Their relevance is that review, documentation, and accountable decisions are already familiar responsibilities in county government. Surveillance deserves a comparably understandable explanation.

My criticism concerns an imbalance: government can gain a greater ability to observe people while residents must work to establish who authorized that power and how it can be challenged. This is an interpretation of the accountability problem, not a finding that officials deliberately evaded the law.

The answers the public still deserves

A useful public explanation would connect these records rather than answer each question in isolation:

  • Authorization: the dated decision, signed agreement, installation approvals, and legal basis claimed for them.
  • Ordinance applicability: how Ordinance 2022-04 applies to the specific installations, including the basis for any claimed exception.
  • Information access: who can search or receive records, the applicable retention and sharing rules, and who audits compliance.
  • Continuing control: who can change, decline to renew, or terminate the arrangement and how residents can challenge its use.

The fact that a particular document is not included in this article does not establish that it does not exist. These are requests for a coherent public account, not unsupported findings of missing records or misconduct.

I oppose broad surveillance of ordinary travel. Holding that position does not excuse imprecision about the law or the evidence. It makes accuracy more important, because the public deserves an argument it can inspect.

Government should be able to explain and substantiate its authority to observe people with at least the clarity it expects from the people it observes.
