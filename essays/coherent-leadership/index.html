Coherent Leadership: Building Systems That Do Not Depend on Human Exhaustion

An exploration of coherent leadership and the ongoing development of systems that preserve human agency, institutional memory, and accountable governance.

November 25, 2026 Essay Brendon R. Coleman
Back to essays Research

For too long, we have mistaken the capacity to endure dysfunction for the capacity to lead.

We celebrate the individual who works longer, carries more responsibility, anticipates every failure, and holds together what would otherwise fall apart. We admire their resilience without adequately questioning why such extraordinary resilience is continually required.

Recently, I encountered an article by Vicki Phillips titled The Power of Coherent Leadership. Although written within the context of education, its central argument speaks directly to something I have been attempting to bring into reality through my own work.

Phillips writes:

It will be built by leaders who can create coherence.

This distinction between heroic leadership and coherent leadership deserves consideration far beyond the educational system.

It challenges the very architecture of how we work, organize, govern, and build.

The Hidden Cost of Heroic Leadership

A system that requires extraordinary human endurance to maintain ordinary functionality is a structurally deficient system.

This does not diminish the value of endurance. There are circumstances in which sacrifice, perseverance, and exceptional personal effort are necessary.

But sacrifice should not become a permanent substitute for structural improvement.

My experience in industrial environments has given me a practical perspective on this distinction. When an operator continually compensates for a recurring mechanical or procedural deficiency, production may continue, but the underlying problem remains unresolved.

The competence of the operator can actually conceal the inadequacy of the system.

Organizations can function in much the same way. Their most capable individuals absorb fragmentation, communication failures, institutional forgetfulness, and poorly defined responsibilities.

Eventually, the organization becomes dependent upon the very exhaustion it celebrates.

What happens when that individual is no longer present?

If everything falls apart, we must question whether we built a functioning system or simply found someone capable of carrying a broken one.

Coherence Is Not Conformity

Coherent leadership is not the centralization of authority, the elimination of disagreement, or the demand that everyone think alike.

A coherent system allows distinct participants to act with meaningful independence while remaining connected to a recognizable purpose.

Its participants understand what they are doing, why they are doing it, what authority they possess, and where their responsibilities end.

This requires alignment between several fundamental elements:

  • Purpose and execution.
  • Authority and accountability.
  • Knowledge and decision-making.
  • Individual agency and collective responsibility.
  • Present action and long-term consequences.

When these relationships become disconnected, human beings are forced to compensate.

When these relationships are made coherent, human capacity becomes available for discernment, creativity, development, and meaningful participation.

The objective is not to produce more compliant people.

It is to produce environments in which people can exercise greater responsibility without being consumed by unnecessary friction.

Bringing Coherence Into Reality

Much of my current work can be understood as an attempt to translate this philosophy into operational architecture.

Through my development of Signal Agent, I am exploring how artificial intelligence, structured knowledge, governance, and persistent organizational memory can work together without requiring the surrender of human judgment.

I am not interested in simply automating more tasks.

I am interested in addressing the fragmentation that makes those tasks unnecessarily difficult to coordinate in the first place.

An organization should not have to rediscover its purpose every time a new conversation begins. It should not lose its reasoning when a document is misplaced, a session ends, or a different participant assumes responsibility.

Knowledge should accumulate without becoming inaccessible.

Decisions should remain traceable.

Authority should remain explicit.

Work should be capable of continuing without requiring one individual to reconstruct the entire operational context from memory.

These principles are informing several areas of my development work.

Persistent Organizational Memory

I am developing methods for preserving source material, decisions, research, creative work, and operational history in structured environments.

The intention is to make accumulated knowledge available for future reasoning rather than allowing it to disappear into disconnected files and conversations.

Memory becomes infrastructure rather than an individual burden.

Governed Artificial Intelligence

AI systems should operate within identifiable boundaries of authority.

An agent may possess the technical capacity to perform an action without possessing the authorization to perform it.

I am working with explicit approval boundaries, evidence preservation, independent review, and verifiable state transitions as mechanisms for maintaining this distinction.

The aim is to increase operational capacity without dissolving human responsibility.

Continuity Without Dependency

A mature system should preserve enough context for another qualified participant to understand what has happened, why it happened, and what remains unresolved.

This is why documentation, reproducibility, and recovery procedures are integral to my work rather than administrative afterthoughts.

The ultimate measure of leadership cannot be how desperately a system needs its leader.

It must also include what remains possible in their absence.

Purpose-Driven Coordination

Through projects such as Jesus Is King and my broader content development infrastructure, I am exploring how distinct activities can remain connected to a foundational purpose.

Research, writing, software development, education, creative production, and public participation need not exist as isolated endeavors.

They can become connected expressions of a coherent mission while retaining their individual boundaries and requirements.

These systems remain under development. Their value must ultimately be demonstrated through operation, testing, and their consequences for the people who interact with them.

A compelling architecture is not yet proof of a functioning institution.

Artificial Intelligence Must Not Become the New Heroic Leader

There is a danger in attempting to solve human exhaustion through artificial intelligence.

We could simply transfer the burden of holding fragmented systems together from an exhausted human being to an increasingly capable machine.

The underlying fragmentation would remain.

An AI agent that compensates for every organizational deficiency may make that organization appear more effective while concealing its structural weaknesses.

That is not the transformation I am pursuing.

Artificial intelligence should help expose fragmentation, preserve knowledge, coordinate authorized action, and reduce unnecessary cognitive burden.

It should not become an unquestionable authority or an indispensable intermediary between human beings and their own responsibilities.

The objective is not artificial intelligence governing humanity.

The objective is humanity developing the capacity to govern increasingly powerful systems coherently.

The Spiritual Foundation

For me, this understanding of leadership is inseparable from my faith.

In Exodus 18, Moses attempts to carry the burden of judging the people himself. Jethro observes the arrangement and tells him:

Thou wilt surely wear away, both thou, and this people that is with thee: for this thing is too heavy for thee; thou art not able to perform it thyself alone.

— Exodus 18:18, KJV

The response is not to abandon responsibility but to restructure its distribution.

The burden is shared. Responsibility is established at appropriate levels. Moses remains accountable for his particular calling without becoming the sole mechanism through which every ordinary matter must pass.

Likewise, in 1 Corinthians 12, Paul describes a body consisting of many members, possessing distinct functions while remaining part of one whole.

Unity does not require uniformity.

Interdependence does not require the destruction of individuality.

Leadership, understood through this lens, is not the accumulation of control. It is the stewardship of conditions through which others can faithfully exercise their own capacities.

I believe our technological and institutional architectures should reflect this understanding of human dignity.

Measuring Leadership Differently

Perhaps we should begin evaluating leadership through a different set of questions.

  • Does knowledge survive the departure of the person who possesses it?
  • Can participants exercise judgment without constant supervision?
  • Are responsibilities and authority clearly defined?
  • Can mistakes be identified and corrected without concealing their occurrence?
  • Does the system preserve its purpose under pressure?
  • Does participation develop human capacity or progressively diminish it?
  • Can the organization continue functioning when its leader rests?

These questions reveal dimensions of leadership that productivity metrics alone cannot capture.

A system may be highly productive while consuming the very people upon whom its productivity depends.

That is not a condition I wish to perpetuate.

From Endurance to Architecture

I have not completed this work.

I am still learning how to embody these principles personally, how to implement them technically, and how to test whether the structures I develop genuinely preserve human agency.

There are contradictions to confront, assumptions to challenge, and methods to refine.

But the direction is clear.

I want to build systems that remember without controlling, coordinate without coercing, and assist without replacing human judgment.

I want to build structures in which endurance is available for meaningful sacrifice rather than continually spent compensating for preventable dysfunction.

I want to develop coherence not merely as a philosophical ideal, but as something observable, testable, reproducible, and transferable.

The future should not depend upon finding increasingly extraordinary individuals willing to carry increasingly fragmented institutions.

We should be building institutions worthy of the people who inhabit them.

Leadership should leave behind capacity, not dependency.
