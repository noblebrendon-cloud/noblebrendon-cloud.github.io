How a Shirt Release Became a Method I Could Use Again

How the interruptions and corrections in a shirt release became a process worth preserving

November 5, 2026 Personal Essay Brendon R. Coleman
Back to essays Read the full method

I began with an image I thought would make a funny shirt. The first practical question was about cropping it: remove the extra white area and keep the part that belonged on the front. I wanted a short path from something I had noticed to something I could put in my store.

That was the scale of the idea at the beginning. I had a graphic, a possible shirt, and a reason to move while the moment still mattered.

I was working with ChatGPT/Codex and connected tools. I asked it to look at how we had handled the process before and start from the first step. I wanted the work to move, with confirmation where a real approval was needed. I also wanted the image in a form I could download. Those requests were ordinary, but each one exposed another part of what a release actually required.

The process stalled around the Printful login. There was work we could describe and prepare, and work that depended on getting into the correct account. The distinction became immediate once we were trying to complete the task. Knowing what should happen next did not supply the access needed to do it.

While that was being worked through, I changed the shirt idea. I supplied a different graphic: a red mask above the words “REMEMBER REMEMBER THE 5TH OF NOVEMBER” against a black background. I made the direction explicit: “I want this on the front of the shirt and that's the graphic.”

That change mattered beyond the image itself. The process needed to recognize which concept was current, keep useful work from the earlier attempt, and avoid carrying the wrong artwork or assumptions into the new product. A conversation can move quickly from one idea to another. The work being done in external systems needs to stay attached to the right one.

I also had a deadline. I wanted the shirt published to Shopify so I could begin posting about it before the end of the fifth. That gave the task a concrete finish: a product someone could actually open through a link.

During the change of direction, I asked for all of this to be recorded. I was already thinking about calling the process again for another shirt. If we had to work through the access, image preparation, product setup, and publication once, I wanted that effort to remain useful afterward.

That request is where the method began to take shape.

Recording the work meant preserving more than a transcript. A later session would need to know which graphic had been selected, what had been changed for production, which store was intended, what permission had been given, and what had actually been completed. It would also need to know where to resume.

I could imagine returning with another image and asking for the same process. For that to mean anything, “the same process” had to become more specific. It needed inputs, decisions, checks, and a record of the result.

The shirt itself continued to supply those details. The selected image needed to fit the print area at an acceptable size. The provider preview needed inspection. The listing needed to show the printed front. The variants, prices, and description needed to agree with the configuration we intended to publish.

Then a particularly useful problem appeared. The description in Shopify’s administration screen was correct, but the public storefront displayed text from another shirt.

From inside the product record, part of the work looked finished. From the page a visitor would see, it was wrong. The template was carrying content that did not belong to the new release.

We corrected the new product’s template assignment and checked the public page again. The release also included adding a variant to the cart, checking what appeared, and removing the test addition afterward.

That experience gave the workflow a concrete definition of completion. It needed to reach the public result and inspect it. A successful operation inside one system was only one piece of evidence.

The same discipline affected how the workflow handled interruptions. If a publication request loses its response, the provider may still be working. A later session needs to inspect the existing state before trying again. Otherwise, an attempt to recover can create a second product.

We included that situation in an offline exercise. The assistant received a pending publication, missing product identifiers, and existing authorization for the matching concept. The useful behavior was to preserve the pending state and reconcile it before another write. The exercise gave us a way to inspect the recovery logic without publishing another shirt.

As the work accumulated, my requests became more explicit. I asked to make it a repeatable workflow that I could call again for a spontaneous idea. I called it Trend Chaser.

The name reflected the original use: something appears, I have an idea for a shirt, and I want to act while it is relevant. Building the workflow made clear how much continuity that kind of quick action requires. The faster I want to move, the more useful it is to know what has already been decided and checked.

I also wanted the workflow to remember authorization accurately. If I had already asked for this shirt to be published to this store, an interruption should not erase that instruction. At the same time, a new concept, an announcement, or a purchase could change the scope. Keeping those boundaries in the record would let the work continue without treating every action as an unanswered question.

Once the personal workflow existed, I asked how to publish it on Zenodo. That led to creating a GitHub repository and preparing a public version.

This was another translation step. My working record contained information needed for my release. Somebody else needed a procedure they could configure for their own account. The public package therefore included instructions, blank templates, recovery notes, a synthetic example, and an account of what had been tested. The private store records and the shirt artwork stayed outside it.

Version 1.0.0 was published on GitHub and archived on Zenodo. The archive gave that version a stable reference. It also made it possible to point to the actual files behind the description of the workflow.

I wanted the claims to stay close to the work. We had one completed release in my store and an offline recovery exercise. That provided useful evidence about the process we had followed. It did not establish physical print quality, delivery performance, or successful use in every other account.

Those limits belong beside the method because another person needs them when deciding what to test. They also leave room for the next version to improve through further use.

Looking back, the sequence is what interests me. An image led to a shirt idea. The attempt to release it exposed access problems, changes of direction, and differences between configured data and visible results. Recording those details made them available as instructions. Preparing those instructions for someone else made me separate the reusable procedure from the circumstances of my own store.

The method grew through working on the task. Each correction gave the next attempt a more informed starting point.

When the repository and archive were finished, I said I hoped it could be helpful to someone. I still think that is the appropriate measure to leave open. Someone might use the entire workflow. Someone might take only the reminder to inspect the public description or preserve a checkpoint before leaving a blocked session.

I would be glad if a small part of the record helped them avoid repeating a problem we had already encountered. I would also be interested in the point where their experience required a better rule.

The shirt gave me a reason to do the work. Recording it gave me a way to return. Publishing the method made that return available to someone else.
