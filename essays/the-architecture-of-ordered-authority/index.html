The Architecture of Ordered Authority

What Jesus teaches about power, stewardship, mediation, accountability, and what remains upstream.

August 27, 2026 Research Essay
Back to essays Research

Authority is one of those words that becomes less clear the longer we use it.

A government has authority. A parent has authority. A teacher has authority. A pastor has authority. A judge has authority. An employer has authority. An institution has authority. A software system may even be authorized to perform actions on someone’s behalf.

But these are obviously not identical things.

A teacher does not possess the authority of a judge. A steward does not possess the authority of an owner. A messenger does not become the source of the message merely because the message has been entrusted to him.

The distinction seems simple.

In practice, however, much of human conflict begins precisely where these boundaries become confused.

The steward begins acting like the owner. The representative begins acting like the source. The servant becomes a ruler over those he was meant to serve. The institution begins treating delegated authority as self-originating authority. The mediator no longer merely mediates. It begins to substitute itself for what was upstream.

I have increasingly come to think of this as a problem of ordered authority.

And some of the clearest material for thinking about it comes from Jesus Himself.

Authority Can Be Real Without Being Sovereign

One of the first distinctions that matters is this:

Authority ≠ Sovereignty

A person can possess genuine authority without being the ultimate source of that authority.

Jesus makes this distinction unexpectedly clear during His exchange with Pilate. Pilate possesses real political and coercive authority. Jesus does not pretend that Pilate has no power over the proceedings taking place around Him.

“You would have no authority over me if it had not been given you from above.” — John 19:11

The point is not that Pilate’s authority is imaginary. The point is that it is not self-originating.

Something can be real, operational, consequential, and institutionally recognized while still remaining downstream from something greater.

Possession of authority does not imply possession of sovereignty.

That distinction matters wherever one actor is entrusted to act within a domain that actor did not create and does not ultimately own.

The Pattern of Sending

The Gospel of John repeatedly describes Jesus in terms of being sent by the Father.

Jesus speaks the Father’s words. He performs the Father’s work. He carries out the mission for which He was sent.

Yet the same Gospel emphatically refuses to reduce Jesus to an ordinary messenger. John begins with the preexistent Logos. Jesus gives life, exercises judgment, receives honor, reveals the Father, and speaks of a unique mutual relationship with Him.

Any serious account therefore has to preserve both realities:

Jesus is sent.

And:

Jesus is not reducible to an ordinary derivative agent.

That distinction is crucial.

Jesus Christ should not simply be placed in the same category as an angel, prophet, pastor, government official, or human representative.

Yet the way Jesus exercises authority during His mission gives us a remarkable pattern of faithfulness to what is upstream.

In John 12:49, Jesus says that He has not spoken simply from Himself, but according to what the Father who sent Him commanded.

In John 13:20, Jesus extends the pattern: receiving the one Jesus sends is connected to receiving Jesus, and receiving Jesus is connected to receiving the One who sent Him.

John 17:18 makes the pattern even more explicit: as the Father sent Jesus into the world, Jesus sends His disciples into the world. John 20:21 repeats it after the resurrection.

This gives us a real source-and-sending structure without collapsing Father, Son, and disciple into the same identity.

Representation ≠ Identity

A representative may truly act on behalf of another without becoming the other.

Stewardship Is Not Ownership

Jesus returns repeatedly to the language of entrusted property.

Masters leave servants responsible for households. Landowners entrust vineyards. Resources are given to servants. Managers receive responsibility. And eventually the owner returns.

The steward is not powerless. Quite the opposite. A steward may possess substantial operational authority. The steward decides. The steward manages. The steward acts.

But there is one boundary the steward cannot legitimately cross:

Steward ≠ Owner

The parable of the wicked tenants makes this particularly vivid (Mark 12:1–12; Matthew 21:33–46; Luke 20:9–19).

The vineyard is placed under the tenants’ control. They work it. They occupy it. They possess practical control over what happens inside it.

But when the owner sends servants to receive what belongs to him, the tenants reject them. Eventually the owner sends his son. The tenants decide that if they kill the heir, the inheritance can become theirs.

The failure is more than bad management.

It is a transformation of identity:

Stewardship → Claimed Ownership

Operational possession becomes a claim to sovereignty.

This gives us a principle with implications far beyond the vineyard:

Exercising control over an entrusted domain does not make the steward the sovereign owner of that domain.

Greater Authority Means Greater Accountability

Modern intuitions about power often run in the opposite direction. The more powerful the person becomes, the fewer people are able to question them. Authority rises while accountability falls.

Jesus describes something very different.

In Luke 12:42–48, the servant who has been placed over the household remains accountable to the master who entrusted the household to him. The master’s temporary absence does not dissolve the servant’s responsibility.

“Everyone to whom much was given, of him much will be required.” — Luke 12:48

Entrustment ↑ → Accountability ↑

Notice what does not follow:

Entrustment ↑ ↛ Sovereignty ↑

The steward does not become less accountable because the steward possesses more responsibility. The opposite happens. Greater entrustment creates greater obligation.

Jesus Does Not Abolish Authority — He Reorders It

When Jesus discusses leadership among His disciples, He does not simply say that no one should exercise authority.

Instead He attacks a particular orientation of authority.

In Mark 10:42–45, Jesus points to rulers who “lord it over” others and contrasts their pattern with the one His followers are supposed to embody. Greatness becomes service. Leadership becomes responsibility. The one who would be first becomes servant.

Jesus then grounds that pattern in Himself: the Son of Man came not to be served, but to serve.

John 13 makes the point even more striking. Jesus explicitly acknowledges Himself as Teacher and Lord. Then He washes His disciples’ feet.

The act therefore cannot mean:

Service = No Authority

Jesus has just affirmed His authority.

The transformation is instead:

Authority → Service

rather than:

Authority → Domination

This matters because criticism of corrupted authority can easily become criticism of authority itself. Jesus appears to do something more precise. He does not abolish ordered responsibility. He rejects its conversion into domination.

When Mediation Becomes Capture

These patterns suggest a broader category.

I call it mediator capture.

A mediator is anything positioned between a source and something downstream. A mediator may teach, represent, translate, execute, administer, govern, witness, preserve, or coordinate.

Mediation is not inherently corrupt. It is necessary in almost every complex human system.

The corruption begins when the mediator starts acquiring functions that belong somewhere else.

Stewardship → Ownership

Representation → Substitution

Delegation → Self-Authorization

Service → Domination

Authority → Claimed Sovereignty

Assistance → Agency Capture

In each case the problem is not merely that the mediator acts. The problem is that the mediator begins claiming more than was legitimately entrusted.

Scripture does not use the phrase mediator capture. The phrase is mine. The question is whether it accurately names a pattern already visible in the text.

So far, I think it does.

Faithful Mediation Preserves What Is Upstream

This leads to a larger possibility.

Perhaps legitimate mediation has identifiable invariants.

A message can change language without changing its source. A teacher can explain without becoming the truth. A steward can administer without becoming the owner. A servant can exercise responsibility without becoming sovereign. A representative can act without becoming identical to the one represented.

The form may change. The relationship must remain recognizable.

This gives us something like:

Source → Mediation → Transformation

where legitimate transformation preserves the things that must remain connected across the transition: source, identity, purpose, scope, responsibility, and accountability.

I would describe that more generally as source fidelity.

Not blind repetition. Not micromanagement. Not abolition of downstream agency.

Fidelity.

Ordered Agency Does Not Require Micromanagement

This distinction is particularly important.

Authority can constrain legitimate action without scripting every individual action.

A faithful steward must make decisions. A disciple who is sent must actually go. A servant entrusted with resources must decide how to use them.

The presence of discretion does not automatically imply sovereignty.

Agency ≠ Sovereignty

and:

Order ≠ Micromanagement

A well-ordered system can contain genuine subordinate agency. The downstream actor may possess discretion, judgment, responsibility, creativity, and initiative without acquiring the right to redefine the source, purpose, or boundaries of the authority entrusted to it.

This idea still requires further scholarly development before I would call it a biblical doctrine. But structurally, it fits remarkably well with Jesus’ repeated pattern of teaching, forming, commissioning, and sending people who must then actually act.

Jesus Christ Is Upstream of the Work

This is also where these questions become personal for me.

I did not begin with software architecture and later decide that biblical language would make it more interesting.

The questions came first.

What is true? Who possesses authority? What makes authority legitimate? What happens when a mediator begins taking the place of its source? What must survive a transformation? Who remains responsible for judgment? How can mediation help another person without capturing that person’s agency?

Those questions existed in my theological and spiritual reasoning before they became technical architecture.

Jesus Christ is upstream of the work.

I do not mean that every technical mechanism I build can be deduced from a Bible verse.

I do not mean that Jesus taught software architecture.

And I do not mean that every structural analogy between theology and technology proves theological causation.

I mean something more basic.

My relationship to Christ shaped the questions. The questions shaped the way I learned to see problems. And those patterns eventually appeared again when I began building systems.

The architecture did not create the questions.

The questions became architecture.

Toward a General Architecture of Ordered Authority

I am calling the emerging field:

General Architecture of Ordered Authority

The name is intentionally broader than derivative authority.

Derivative authority describes an important class of authority: a source authorizes another actor.

But not every form of authority can be reduced to the same relationship. Divine sovereignty is not pastoral authority. Christological authority is not angelic authority. Judicial authority is not teaching authority. Witness is not judgment. Execution is not ownership.

So the theory must preserve distinctions rather than erase them.

A preliminary definition is:

Ordered authority is authority exercised in continuing fidelity to the source, purpose, scope, responsibility, and accountability that make its exercise legitimate.

Its corruption occurs when these relationships collapse:

Disordered authority emerges when an actor claims ownership, sovereignty, scope, identity, judgment, or legitimacy that does not properly belong to it.

These are still theoretical formulations. They are not quotations from Scripture.

The broader scholarly work behind them remains in development, particularly around Jewish agency, Second Temple literature, early Christian authority structures, angelic order, ecclesiology, and competing interpretations of delegated authority.

That qualification matters.

I am interested in whether the architecture survives examination, not in protecting it from examination.

But enough has survived already to make the central question worth asking:

What is upstream, what has actually been entrusted downstream, and what must remain true for the resulting action to remain legitimate?

That question applies to more domains than theology.

It applies to institutions. Governments. Teachers. Churches. Technological systems. Artificial intelligence. And ultimately to every place where one actor is trusted to act within a reality it did not create.

Because authority does not become legitimate merely because it can act.

Power does not make itself sovereign.

Control does not create ownership.

Representation does not create sourcehood.

And the moment a steward forgets that he is a steward, the architecture has already begun to break.
