The Numbers Are Not the Mission

On faithfulness, visibility, and the unseen fruit of obedience

October 25, 2026 Scripture-rooted Reflection Brendon R. Coleman
Back to essays

There's something strange about living in a time when we can measure how many people encounter our words, yet know so little about what happens after they hear them.

We can see views, reactions, shares, watch times, retention rates, and the percentage of people who discover us without ever having followed us.

We can measure how far a message travels.

But can we measure how deeply it lands?

Lately, I've been watching my creative work reach people I have never met. Videos about faith, obedience, perseverance, and the internal work of the Holy Spirit are finding their way beyond the community I've already built.

And I find that encouraging.

Not simply because the numbers are increasing, but because these are things I've spent years living, wrestling with, studying, and trying to articulate.

There's something meaningful about finally finding the words for something God has been teaching you privately, then discovering those words have significance to someone else.

But this process has also brought me back to a question I believe matters far beyond social media:

How do we measure the significance of something whose greatest consequences may never be visible to us?

The temptation to measure what matters by what we can count

We live in a culture increasingly organized around measurable performance.

Attention becomes a metric. Influence becomes a number. Success becomes a dashboard.

And without realizing it, we can begin confusing visibility with value.

A message that reaches a hundred thousand people appears more significant than one that reaches a hundred.

But what if one person among those hundred hears something that changes the direction of their life?

What if a word of encouragement reaches someone who was ready to give up?

What if a testimony plants a seed that doesn't begin growing until years later?

What if the person who needed to hear you never comments, never follows, and never tells you what your words meant?

The analytical systems available to us are remarkably capable of measuring distribution.

But distribution is not the same as transformation.

An algorithm can tell me whether someone watched my video.

It cannot tell me whether that person went home and forgave someone.

It cannot tell me whether a struggling believer found the courage to pray again.

It cannot tell me whether something I said became part of a conversation between that person and God.

And that distinction matters.

Because when we begin treating the measurable as the entirety of what is meaningful, we risk overlooking the very things that make our work worth doing.

The biblical accounting of faithfulness

Paul addressed something remarkably relevant in his first letter to the Corinthians.

"I have planted, Apollos watered; but God gave the increase."

— 1 Corinthians 3:6 (KJV)

In context, Paul was addressing divisions among believers who were attaching themselves to different teachers.

His response redirected their attention away from the prominence of individual workers and toward the God responsible for the growth.

One plants. Another waters.

But neither can claim ultimate ownership of what grows.

I find that profoundly liberating.

It means I can take responsibility for what I've been given to do without pretending that I control every consequence of doing it.

I can study carefully.

I can speak truthfully.

I can improve how I communicate.

I can learn from the people who listen.

I can even examine the data to understand whether my message is reaching people.

But I cannot manufacture the internal transformation of another human being.

And I shouldn't pretend that I can.

My responsibility is not to control the harvest.

It is to be faithful with what has been entrusted to me.

That doesn't guarantee every seed will grow. It doesn't mean every message is wise, every effort fruitful, or every conviction beyond correction.

It means that the visible outcome is not the only legitimate measure of whether an act was faithful.

The work nobody sees

Some of the most consequential things God has done in my life happened where no audience could see them.

There were no cameras.

No applause.

No engagement metrics.

There was pain. There were questions. There were seasons of uncertainty, perseverance, study, and prayer.

There were things I couldn't explain while I was living through them.

And over time, some of those experiences began finding their way into words.

That's a large part of why I create now.

I'm not trying to invent a message that will perform well.

I'm trying to articulate something that has already been taking shape in my life.

That difference is important to me.

Because authentic expression begins before an audience ever encounters it.

It begins in the quiet places where our beliefs meet actual experience.

Where convictions are tested.

Where obedience costs something.

Where we must decide whether something is true and worth living by even when nobody is watching.

Jesus Himself warned against performing acts of righteousness merely to be seen by others in Matthew 6.

The problem wasn't that other people might witness something good.

The problem was making their attention the purpose of the act.

That distinction remains relevant in a world where nearly everything can become content.

The question isn't simply whether people are watching.

It's whether we would still believe, practice, and speak these things if they weren't.

Let the numbers inform the method, not define the mission

I don't believe the answer is to reject measurement.

That would confuse the instrument with the temptation surrounding it.

Metrics can tell us something useful.

They can reveal whether an explanation is understandable, whether an introduction holds attention, whether a longer teaching is reaching new people, or whether a particular format helps communicate an idea more clearly.

Those are legitimate things to learn.

If I genuinely care about communicating something meaningful, I should also care whether I'm communicating it effectively.

But there's a boundary I don't want to cross.

Feedback should help me refine the expression of my convictions, not determine which convictions I'm willing to express.

I can change the length of a video without changing the truth I'm trying to communicate.

I can improve the clarity of a message without making it less honest.

I can study audience behavior without allowing audience approval to become my moral authority.

And I can celebrate growth without allowing growth to become my identity.

In fact, I have real goals for the reach of my creative work.

I would love to see these messages travel farther than they already have.

But the number itself is an abstract target.

The actual purpose is the human encounter on the other side of it.

I don't want a million views merely to possess a million views.

I want to communicate something worth hearing, as faithfully and clearly as I can, while remaining open to what I still have to learn.

The numbers can help me understand the movement of the message.

They cannot establish its truth.

They cannot establish its worth.

And they certainly cannot replace the work of the Spirit.

The possibility of unseen fruit

Galatians 6:9 says:

"And let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not."

— Galatians 6:9 (KJV)

I think about that frequently.

Not as a promise that every project will succeed or that every act of obedience will eventually produce public recognition.

But as encouragement to remain faithful when the evidence of growth is incomplete.

Some seeds take time.

Some are watered by people we will never meet.

Some of the things we do may become significant in ways we never have the privilege of witnessing.

And some outcomes may look nothing like what we expected.

That uncertainty doesn't make faithfulness meaningless.

It makes faithfulness necessary.

There is a particular freedom in doing what you believe is right without requiring immediate confirmation that it mattered.

You stop demanding that every act of obedience produce a receipt.

You begin to recognize that the absence of visible results is not necessarily the absence of meaningful work.

And you learn to hold your expectations with humility.

I may never know how many people were truly helped by something I said.

But I can be responsible for the integrity of what I say.

I can remain willing to correct myself.

I can continue growing in understanding.

And I can refuse to confuse public attention with spiritual authority.

What I want to preserve

As my creative work continues to develop, I want to become more capable.

More articulate.

More disciplined.

More effective at communicating what I've learned.

I want to understand the systems that carry my words into the world.

I want to recognize patterns, evaluate results, and use the tools available to me intelligently.

But I also want to preserve the reason I began.

Because the most important things I have to say were not born from an algorithm.

They came from living.

From suffering.

From studying.

From encountering God in circumstances I would not have chosen.

From discovering that faith is not merely something we explain, but something we are called to live.

And if those experiences can become encouragement for someone else, I'm grateful for every opportunity to communicate them.

Whether that opportunity reaches ten people or ten thousand.

I will keep paying attention to the numbers.

I will keep learning from them.

But I will not ask them to tell me whether my life, my testimony, or my obedience has been worthwhile.

That judgment does not belong to a dashboard.

Let the numbers inform the method, but never define the mission.

Because the measure of faithfulness is not necessarily the visibility of its fruit.

And some of the most important things we plant may grow in places we never get to see.
