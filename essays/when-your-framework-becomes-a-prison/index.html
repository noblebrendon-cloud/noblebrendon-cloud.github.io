When Your Framework Becomes a Prison: Epistemology, Zionism, and the Pursuit of Truth

An examination of ideological categorization, biblical covenant theology, political Zionism, and the pursuit of truth beyond our presuppositions.

November 27, 2026 Essay Brendon R. Coleman
Back to essays

One of the most fascinating things about engaging with people online is observing how people respond when confronted with something that does not fit within their established understanding of reality.

Rather than investigating the reasoning behind a statement, asking questions, or attempting to understand the epistemology from which another person operates, they immediately begin categorizing that individual.

They place you inside a familiar ideological box and proceed to argue against the box.

They are no longer engaging with you. They are engaging with their own construction of you.

I recently encountered an example of this after making the statement that Jesus was not a Zionist and that being Jewish and being a Zionist are not synonymous.

In the ensuing exchange, one participant accused another of replacement theology and suggested that disagreement was the product of terrorist propaganda.

But what fascinates me is how many theological and philosophical assumptions had to be made before those accusations could even be reached.

In the comments I saw, there was no inquiry into the reasoning, no establishment of shared theological premises, and no examination of what the disagreement actually consisted of before those accusations were made.

Just categorization.

Scripture addresses this very disposition:

He that answereth a matter before he heareth it, it is folly and shame unto him.

— Proverbs 18:13, KJV

There is a fundamental difference between possessing knowledge and possessing the capacity to recognize truth beyond the boundaries of your existing knowledge.

You can possess an extensive theological education, memorize Scripture, and articulate an elaborate worldview while simultaneously lacking the humility necessary to examine your own presuppositions.

Biblical Covenant and Political Ideology

And that brings me to the theological substance of the conversation itself.

Recognizing God's covenant with Israel does not necessitate identifying Christ with modern political Zionism.

I do not subscribe to replacement theology. I do not deny God's covenant with Israel, nor do I deny the historical connection of the Jewish people to the land of Israel.

Paul explicitly addresses this in Romans:

I say then, Hath God cast away his people? God forbid.

— Romans 11:1, KJV (excerpt)

But recognizing these things does not require accepting every theological or political conclusion someone derives from them.

The ancient Jewish aspiration to return to Zion and the emergence of modern political Zionism as an organized movement in the nineteenth century are historically related, but they are not identical. The National Library of Israel's historical overview of Zionism distinguishes these developments.

Election and Prophetic Accountability

Furthermore, if we are going to discuss Israel from a biblical perspective, we cannot selectively appropriate the promises of the prophets while neglecting the substance of their warnings.

Consider what God says through Amos:

You only have I known of all the families of the earth: therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities.

— Amos 3:2, KJV

Notice the causal direction of that statement.

God does not say He will hold Israel accountable despite having chosen them.

He says THEREFORE.

Their election was the very reason for their increased accountability.

Now consider Jeremiah:

Trust ye not in lying words, saying, The temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, The temple of the LORD, are these.

— Jeremiah 7:4, KJV

Jeremiah goes on to establish conditions concerning justice, the oppression of strangers, the fatherless, widows, and the shedding of innocent blood.

The presence of the temple was not a substitute for righteousness.

The possession of the land was not a substitute for obedience.

Religious identity was not a substitute for faithfulness to God.

Consider Micah:

They build up Zion with blood, and Jerusalem with iniquity.

— Micah 3:10, KJV

Was Micah an antisemite?

Was Jeremiah advocating replacement theology?

Was Amos an enemy of Israel?

Or were these men so faithful to the God of Israel that they refused to allow national identity, religious institutions, or political authority to supersede His righteousness?

What Does Restoration Mean?

And what of the restoration spoken of in Ezekiel?

A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you:

— Ezekiel 36:26, KJV (excerpt)

And I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes, and ye shall keep my judgments, and do them.

— Ezekiel 36:27, KJV

The prophetic vision certainly includes the gathering of Israel, but it also includes spiritual transformation, obedience, purification, reconciliation, and the presence of God.

Ezekiel 37 describes one shepherd, an everlasting covenant of peace, and God's sanctuary among His people.

Isaiah describes nations beating their swords into plowshares and learning war no more.

Christians may disagree concerning the chronology and fulfillment of these prophecies. Even if one believes that the establishment of modern Israel constitutes a stage of prophetic fulfillment, that does not establish divine endorsement of every political decision made thereafter.

Prophetic significance and moral righteousness are not interchangeable concepts.

God can accomplish His purposes through historical events without sanctifying every human action involved in them.

Read Habakkuk.

The Kingdom of Christ

And then consider Christ Himself:

My kingdom is not of this world: if my kingdom were of this world, then would my servants fight,

— John 18:36, KJV (excerpt)

Christ did not deny His Jewish heritage. He did not deny the prophets. He did not deny the promises of God.

But neither did He permit the political expectations of His contemporaries to determine the nature of His Kingdom.

That distinction matters.

The dignity of Jewish people does not require the denial of Palestinian suffering. Likewise, concern for Palestinian suffering does not justify hatred toward Jewish people.

Righteousness does not change according to the ethnicity, nationality, or political affiliation of the person standing before us.

When the Framework Becomes a Prison

And this brings us back to the original epistemological problem.

When someone cannot conceive of a person recognizing God's covenant with Israel without simultaneously identifying Christ with a modern political ideology, perhaps the limitation lies not within the other person's theology, but within the categories through which they are attempting to understand it.

A false dichotomy does not become true merely because you cannot conceive of a third position.

This applies far beyond Zionism.

We see it in politics, theology, psychology, interpersonal relationships, and virtually every environment in which human beings become emotionally invested in their understanding of reality.

We confuse our interpretation of truth with truth itself.

We defend conclusions without examining their premises.

We attribute motives without investigating reasoning.

We mistake disagreement for hostility.

And eventually, we become incapable of recognizing anything that exists outside the framework through which we have learned to perceive the world.

Our frameworks should serve our pursuit of truth. Truth should never be made subordinate to our frameworks.

Prove all things; hold fast that which is good.

— 1 Thessalonians 5:21, KJV

Notice that Scripture does not instruct us to fear examination. It instructs us to practice discernment.

I am not obligated to accept your entire theological framework simply because I recognize a truth contained within it. Nor am I obligated to reject every truth you possess because I disagree with your conclusions.

Truth is not the exclusive possession of an ideological camp.

And as a Christian, I refuse to make any contemporary political ideology the governing authority through which I interpret Jesus Christ.

Christ is the authority through which I examine political ideologies.

Not the other way around.

The prophets demonstrated their faithfulness to Israel not by defending everything Israel did, but by remaining faithful to God even when that faithfulness required confronting Israel itself.

May we possess that same courage, humility, and discernment.

Jesus Christ is King.
