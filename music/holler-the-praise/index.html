A ten-song devotional journey

Holler the Praise

A cry becomes cleansing. Cleansing becomes a road. The road learns to listen, endure, depend, surrender — and finally praise.

January 25, 2026 10 tracks NobleWorksLLC
The album

Before it was a release, it was a place I kept returning to.

Holler the Praise grew from a simple posture: make worship from the world as it was actually being lived.

The project first took shape under the working language Holler Gospel. The word carried two meanings at once: the Appalachian hollow, and the act of crying something out. I wanted Christian worship at the center, but carried through folk, roots, country-blues, fiddle, stomp, wind, river, timber and fire instead of polished contemporary-worship conventions.

Once the ten songs are read together, another shape becomes visible. The album does not stay in one spiritual posture. It moves. A wanderer cries out. He goes down to the water. He keeps watch by a fire. He puts his boots back on the road. He learns to hear God in quietness, then to trust Him when quietness becomes storm. Hunger exposes dependence. Creation becomes praise. What remains is offered like ash and ember. Only then does the record arrive at its final hallelujah.

This page follows that movement in the album’s published order. The writing below separates the story inside each song from the wider story the ten songs tell together.

The movement: cry → cleansing → watchfulness → walking → listening → endurance → dependence → praise → surrender → hallelujah.

01
The cry

Holler of the Hills

The album opens already in motion: a man has been walking through shadows, carrying burdens, weathering storms, and remembering mercy. The important thing is that the first act is not explanation. It is voice. He hollers.

The landscape immediately becomes part of the worship. Ridge, river, cedar trees, valley and highlands do not sit behind the song as scenery; they answer it. The holler is both the place he stands and the form his praise takes. Sorrow is not erased before praise begins. It is converted into praise while still carrying its grit.

That establishes the album’s grammar: darkness can speak, but mercy speaks louder; the valley is not outside worship; and a wandering heart can still lift its voice toward home.

“Now my sorrow’s turned to singin’,
and my singin’s turned to praise You, Lord.”Holler of the Hills

02
The washing

Riverbed Redemption

The cry from the hills descends to water. The imagery becomes physical: mud on the soul, sins like stones, cracked hands, cold water. Redemption here is not abstract. It is something the song imagines being carried to a river and washed.

But the river does more than clean. It redirects. A drifting heart becomes a path. Hunger becomes bread. Yesterday’s chains are laid down. Grace becomes the bridge between river and ridge.

The record has moved from being heard to being changed. The voice that shouted across the hills now asks to be made clean enough to keep walking.

“From the river to the ridge,
Your grace is my bridge.”Riverbed Redemption

03
The watch

FIRELIGHT PSALMS

Night returns, but now there is a fire inside it. The song was shaped around weary watchfulness: wind through the pines, shadows on cedar bark, wolves in the valley, embers holding against the dark.

The fire becomes a small theology of endurance. Each spark is a prayer. The flame is steady rather than spectacular. Fear is not denied; it is placed beside the flame while trust grows warm enough to sing.

The song also widens worship beyond the song itself: “worship, work, and song.” That line matters to the album’s world. Praise is beginning to move from an isolated cry toward a way of inhabiting ordinary life.

“Teach my heart to burn like this,
In worship, work, and song.”FIRELIGHT PSALMS

04
The road

Boots on the Old Road

Now the album starts walking. The boots are already worn from wandering; the road is not a fantasy of a clean beginning. It is the same pair of feet, carrying their history, choosing a different direction.

The song’s faith is deliberately modest: “I ain’t got much but this old faith.” It does not claim to know where the trail turns. It claims something smaller and harder — willingness to keep pace with God.

The old road also introduces company. Saints and sinners have walked it before. Their echoes join the hills. What began as one man’s holler is slowly discovering a lineage, a path, and a homeward direction.

“I don’t know where the trail will turn,
But I trust Your steady pace.”Boots on the Old Road

05
The whisper

Wind Through the Pines

The album quiets down. God is not encountered here in thunder or fire, but in a voice the heart can hear. Branches bow like prayer. Every sway becomes a sermon. Solitude becomes a place where faith learns how to bend without breaking.

This is an important turn in the record. Earlier songs needed a holler loud enough to cross a valley. This one learns that presence does not always arrive loudly. Discernment becomes part of discipleship.

The repeated request is not for certainty but for nearness: steady my fears, guide my feet, remind me who You are. The road is now being navigated by attention.

“Not in thunder, not in fire,
But in a voice my heart could hear.”Wind Through the Pines

06
The storm

Stormlight in the Holler

Clouds roll in, thunder groans, rain hits the tin roof, and fear runs like a river. The song does not escape the storm; it looks for light inside it.

That is what “stormlight” names. Grace does not wait for the weather to clear. Jesus is described as the light that holds the heart while the night is still happening — steady when the singer cannot stand, guiding without pretending the storm is unreal.

Placed after Wind Through the Pines, the contrast is sharp: the God heard in a whisper is the same God trusted in thunder. Quietness and crisis become two conditions of the same relationship.

“You hold my heart when the night won’t end—
Jesus, You’re my stormlight in the holler.”Stormlight in the Holler

07
The hunger

Hunger of the Hills

The imagery becomes materially plain: a bare pantry, hands worked to the bone, empty fields, toil, want. Physical hunger and spiritual hunger are allowed to occupy the same song.

The central discovery is that emptiness can reveal what appetite cannot satisfy. The world’s promise is called thin; grace is pictured as bread and rain. The prayer changes from “give me what I want” toward “let Your presence be my portion.”

This makes hunger a school of dependence. Scarcity does not become romantic. It becomes a place where the singer learns what he is actually asking to be filled with.

“When all I have is just a prayer,
Your mercy makes me whole.”Hunger of the Hills

08
The ridge

Pine Ridge Praise

By the eighth track, the landscape has changed function. Wind, creek, hills, thunder, fading light and dawn are no longer primarily threats or conditions to endure. They become signs through which praise keeps rising.

The repeated confession is simple: You hear me, You catch me, Your mercy echoes. Help comes from God’s hand. Hope comes around the bend.

The album is beginning to answer its opening holler. The same hills that received the first cry now return an echo of mercy.

“Your mercy echoes through it all—
So I sing my praise on Pine Ridge.”Pine Ridge Praise

09
The offering

Ash and Ember Prayer

The fire imagery from track three returns, but it has changed. Earlier, the fire gave warmth and watchfulness. Here the singer brings himself to it as ash and ember — what remains after burning.

The prayer is one of surrender and purification: take what is left, make it true, shape this soul, breathe new fire. Old fears still howl in the night, and the singer remembers roads where hope felt friendless, but the posture is no longer mainly defensive. It is offering.

The song’s most important claim may be its smallest: even a spark can be given. Weakness is not disqualified from worship. It becomes the material of the prayer.

“If all I have is a spark to give,
Lord, take it—teach my heart to live.”Ash and Ember Prayer

10
The hallelujah

Holler Hallelujah

The finale opens at sunrise. After storms and nights, the voice that began the album alone in the shadows now stands in daylight and calls itself reborn.

The scale expands with every verse. Ridge and river join the song. The valley echoes the name of Jesus. Mountains answer. Heaven joins. The private holler becomes something almost congregational — a front-porch revival carried across the ridges.

But the ending is not triumph detached from the earlier tracks. The hallelujah carries all of them inside it: the mud, cold water, firelight, worn boots, whispering pines, storm, hunger, mercy, ash and ember. The praise has weight because the album let the path remain visible.

“After all the storms and nights I knew,
Lord, I stand reborn in You.”Holler Hallelujah

After the holler

The record ends where it was always trying to go: not out of the hollow, but into praise inside it.

Looking across the ten songs, the recurring landscape never disappears. Hills, rivers, roads, pines, storms, hunger, fire and night remain present all the way through. What changes is the relationship to them. At first they hold the pressure of wandering and fear. By the end they have become places of encounter, dependence, offering and witness.

That is why the album’s final movement does not feel like leaving the holler behind. The holler itself has been transformed. The place that first received a weary cry becomes the place from which hallelujah can finally ring.

The path was never erased. It became part of the praise.
